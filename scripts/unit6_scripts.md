**Rules applied in this unit.** No boundaries, no outline of India, no empire drawn as shaded territory: capitals, cave sites and temples are pins on a schematic physical board. ## Episode 29 — Gupta administration and society

**Inscription tip:** "LAND GRANTED · POWER DIVIDED" · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, texts, travellers · **Timeline ribbon:** c. 320 – 550 CE · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: did land grants create Indian feudalism? · **Exhibit:** a copper-plate land grant · **Label facts:** Provinces, districts, villages · Land grants in copper · Fa-Hien's account

| 0:00 | The Dig | DIG: the brush reveals two copper plates joined by a ring, their inner faces covered in engraved lines. Tag: "A deed, in metal". | The brush uncovers two sheets of copper, joined by a ring and engraved on the inside. This is a legal deed — a grant of land, recorded in metal so it would outlast everybody who signed it. Hundreds of these survive, and they are the single most useful source for how early Indian society was changing. |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: stone, palm-leaf and staff light. | Inscriptions, especially the copper plates. Texts, including law books, which tell us what was supposed to happen rather than what did. And Fa-Hien, a Chinese Buddhist pilgrim who travelled in India in the early fifth century looking for Buddhist texts and wrote down what he observed. |
| 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: a carved administrative tree — the king, then provinces, districts, villages, with the officials named at each level. | The structure, briefly, because questions ask for the terms. The empire divided into provinces, bhuktis, under governors called uparikas. Provinces into districts, vishayas, under vishayapatis. And below that the village, with its headman and local assemblies. Compared with the Mauryas this is a looser arrangement: more was left to local people and to feudatories who acknowledged the Guptas without being administered by them. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved plate being handed over; a village with its revenue marked as flowing to a new holder rather than to the king. | Now the change that matters most. The Guptas and their successors granted land — to Brahmins, to temples, to monasteries, and increasingly to officials in place of salary. The grant usually transferred the revenue of a village, and often the right to administer it, sometimes in perpetuity and free of tax. Follow that through and you can see the consequence: every grant is a piece of the state's income and authority handed permanently to somebody else. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved pair of panels — a traveller with a staff and a writing tablet; a town with rest houses and a hospital tag. | Fa-Hien describes the Ganga plains as peaceful and prosperous. He notes that travel was safe, that charitable rest houses and free hospitals existed, that officials did not demand documents from travellers. He also notes, in passing, that people called chandalas lived outside the towns and had to strike a piece of wood to announce themselves so others could avoid them. Both observations are in the same account, and an honest answer uses both. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: carved panels of society — a guild workshop, a village assembly, a land-grant plate, a law book on a stand. | What else the sources show: guilds remained important in towns; villages ran a good deal of their own affairs; and the law books of this period set out a social order of great rigidity, with the position of women and of the lowest-ranked groups more restricted than in earlier texts. Remember the distinction — the law books are prescriptive. They tell you what their authors wanted, which is evidence of attitudes, not a description of how everybody lived. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "Land grants created an Indian feudalism: a decentralised order of lords, dependent peasants and a weakened state." Right: "The parallel with Europe misleads: trade and towns did not simply collapse, and local power took many forms." | This is one of the big arguments in Indian history. One influential position holds that from the Gupta period land grants produced something properly called Indian feudalism — power and revenue devolving to intermediaries, peasants tied more tightly to the land, trade and towns declining, and the state weakening. The other holds that the European comparison imports assumptions that do not fit, that the evidence for urban decline is mixed, and that what happened is better described as regional states forming. You are not expected to settle it. You are expected to know both sides and the evidence each uses. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: the chisel cuts "LAND GRANTED · POWER DIVIDED", then verdigris fills the grooves. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. Land granted, power divided. Every plate gives a piece of the state away. |

The Rashtrakutas, under rulers including Dhruva, Govinda the Third and Amoghavarsha, were the most frequently successful of the three in the north, and the most interesting at home. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved hillside at Ellora with many cave entrances, then the Kailasa temple standing free in its cut courtyard, architecture only, sanctum dark. | And the Rashtrakutas built Kailasa at Ellora, which is the monument of this episode. Cut downward out of the hillside in the eighth century under Krishna the First, it is a complete temple — tower, hall, gateway, courtyard — excavated rather than constructed. Ellora also holds Buddhist, Jain and Hindu caves side by side on the same hillside, which is worth noticing: three traditions patronised at one site. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved balance with the three powers on it, tipping back and forth; then smaller nameplates appearing around the edges as the three shrink. | So how does it end? Nobody wins. Kannauj changes hands repeatedly; the Rashtrakutas raid north and withdraw south; the Pratiharas hold it longest and then fragment; the Palas decline and revive and decline again. By the tenth century all three are weakening and their own feudatories are becoming independent kingdoms — the Rajput houses in the west, the Chandelas, the Paramaras, and in the Deccan the Chalukyas of Kalyani. The struggle's real outcome is not a winner but a map of regional states. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "The struggle exhausted all three and left the north fragmented and vulnerable." The common account says the three wore each other out, left the north divided, and so prepared the way for later invasions. There is something in it. But the more careful reading points out that each power declined for reasons of its own — succession disputes, pressure from feudatories, regional revolts — that the period produced major temples, literature and learning rather than decay, and that treating two centuries of history as a prelude to someone else's arrival is a poor way to understand it. Use the narrative; do not let it do your thinking. |
