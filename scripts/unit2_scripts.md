# Ancient India, Simply Unearthed — Unit 2: The Vedic age

Episodes 7 to 11. Same format as `sample_ep04.md`: a header line, the seven segments, then the Hindi table.

**Care taken in this unit.** The origins of the Vedic people and the origins of varna are both live scholarly debates and both are politically charged. Every episode here states what the texts say, states what the archaeology shows, keeps the two apart, and puts the competing readings on equal tablets without choosing between them. No deity is drawn; gods are named in narration and shown only through the fire altar and the texts.

It was composed in an archaic form of Sanskrit, and then carried orally, by families of reciters trained to repeat it exactly, syllable by syllable, for many centuries before it was written. That is why its language is older than the manuscripts it survives in. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a schematic physical board, rivers only, no borders. Seven river lines light up. It calls itself the land of seven rivers, Sapta Sindhu, and the rivers it names most often lie in the north-west. The Sindhu is mentioned constantly. The Saraswati is praised as the greatest of rivers. The Ganga appears only once. That pattern tells you roughly where these people were, and in which direction they had not yet gone. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: cattle carved in a long line; a raiding party; a small scale weighing a cow against goods. | Their economy was pastoral. Cattle were the measure of wealth, so completely that the word for a battle in the Rigveda literally means a search for cows. They farmed too, mainly barley, but land was not the thing people counted. Cattle were. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a tribal assembly — figures seated in a ring around a chief who stands, not sits above. Labels: "jana", "raja", "sabha", "samiti". Then a fire altar, with no image of any deity. | And the politics were tribal, not royal. People belonged to a jana, a tribe. Its leader was a raja — but this is a chief, chosen for leadership in war and for protecting cattle, not a king with a territory and a treasury. He was advised, and checked, by assemblies: the sabha, a smaller body, and the samiti, a larger one. He took bali, offerings, but these were voluntary, not taxes. The gods they invoked — Indra, Agni, Varuna — were worshipped at the fire, in the open. There were no temples and no images. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree", two equal tablets. Left: "Migration: language and texts point to Indo-Aryan speakers arriving from Central Asia." Right: "Indigenous development: the material culture shows continuity, with no evidence of invasion." | Now the question everyone asks, and the one where opinion runs hottest. Where did the Vedic people come from? One body of scholarship, resting mainly on language — on how Sanskrit relates to other Indo-European languages — argues that Indo-Aryan speakers moved into the subcontinent from Central Asia over a long period. Another argues for development within the subcontinent, pointing to continuity in the material record and to the absence of any evidence of invasion. Two things are worth separating. Almost all scholars now reject the old idea of a violent invasion destroying Harappan cities. The narrower question of migration is genuinely open and actively argued. This series states both positions and takes neither. |

First: a collection of more than a thousand hymns, in ten books, the oldest text composed in India. The Rigveda. Second: the two assemblies that advised and checked the chief. The sabha and the samiti. Third: the land of seven rivers, where most of the Rigveda is set. Sapta Sindhu. In Mains, a standard question asks you to describe Early Vedic polity. The marks are in the word chief: no territory, no standing army, no regular tax, and assemblies that mattered. |

## Episode 8 — The Later Vedic period

**Inscription tip:** "IRON EAST · PLOUGH AND SACRIFICE" · **Sources lit:** texts, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** c. 1000 – 600 BCE · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: how closely does Painted Grey Ware map onto the Later Vedic people? · **Exhibit:** a Painted Grey Ware bowl · **Label facts:** Iron and the move east · Big sacrifices make big kings · Assemblies fade

| Time | Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | The Dig | DIG: the brush reveals a thin, grey, finely made bowl with simple painted designs. Tag: "Painted Grey Ware". | The brush finds a bowl: thin-walled, grey, carefully made, with simple designs painted on it. Archaeologists call it Painted Grey Ware, and they find it across the plains between the Ganga and the Yamuna. It belongs to the centuries when the world of the Rigveda turned into something quite different. |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: the palm-leaf icon and the trowel both light. | Now we have two sources that talk to each other. The later Vedic texts tell us what people said they were doing. Painted Grey Ware and iron tools tell us where they were and how they lived. Historians use each to test the other. |
| 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: an iron axe and an iron ploughshare carved large; behind them, forest giving way to cleared field. | The first change is iron. The texts call it the dark metal. Iron axes clear forest that copper tools could not, and iron-shod ploughs break heavier soil. This is what lets people move off the light soils of the north-west and into the thickly wooded, far more fertile plains of the Ganga and the Yamuna. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a schematic river board, the centre of gravity shifting eastward; settlements thicken along the doab. | So the centre of gravity moves east, into the doab. The Rigveda barely mentions the Ganga; later Vedic texts are full of it. Settlements get larger and stay put. And once people stay put and grow a surplus, two things follow that change everything: land starts to matter more than cattle, and somebody starts collecting. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a king raised on a platform; beside him a fire altar of layered bricks; three labels rise — rajasuya, ashvamedha, vajapeya. | The chief becomes a king. You can watch it happen through the rituals. The rajasuya, a royal consecration. The ashvamedha, the horse sacrifice, where a horse is let loose for a year and the land it crosses unchallenged is claimed. The vajapeya, a chariot race the king is arranged to win. These are enormous, expensive ceremonies, and their purpose is political: they turn a leader into a figure of a different order, and they need priests to run them. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: the assembly ring from the last episode, now smaller and off to one side, fading; the king's household and officials grow instead. | And as the king rises, the assemblies fall. The samiti is mentioned less and less. The sabha narrows into something closer to a council of notables. Tribal units, the janas, begin to harden into territories, the janapadas, named after the people who hold them — and a territory is a thing a king can rule, tax and lose. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "Painted Grey Ware marks Later Vedic settlement: the spread matches the texts closely." Right: "A pottery style is not a people: the fit is approximate and should not be pressed." | A caution, because this one catches people out. Painted Grey Ware is found roughly where the later Vedic texts suggest these people were, and roughly when. Some scholars treat that match as strong enough to identify the two. Others warn that a pottery style travels through trade and imitation, and that equating pots with peoples has misled archaeology before. The overlap is real; the identification is an argument, not a fact. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: the chisel cuts "IRON EAST · PLOUGH AND SACRIFICE", then verdigris fills the grooves. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. Iron east. Plough and sacrifice. |
| 3:20 | Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three carved cards — a thin grey bowl; a horse with a year-long path behind it; an iron ploughshare. Then a Mains card. | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. First: thin grey pottery with painted designs, found across the Ganga-Yamuna doab. Painted Grey Ware. Second: a sacrifice in which a horse is let loose for a year, and the land it crosses unchallenged is claimed. The ashvamedha. Third: the metal the texts call dark, which cleared the eastern forests. Iron. In Mains, a frequent question asks how iron changed early Indian society. Do not stop at better tools: go to forest clearing, surplus, settled territory, taxation and kingship. |

Next gallery: the texts themselves, and how they fit together. To catch it when it's out, subscribe to Gyaan-X Studios. |

| 1:50 | दो बातें इन संस्कृतियों को महत्वपूर्ण बनाती हैं। पहली, दक्षिण में लोहा जल्दी आता है — कुछ जगहों पर उपमहाद्वीप में कहीं भी जितना जल्दी — जिससे यह सरल कहानी उलझ जाती है कि तकनीक किसी एक केंद्र से बाहर की ओर फैली। दूसरी, ये समुदाय ठीक उसी क्षेत्र में खेती, पशुपालन और धातुकर्म कर रहे थे जहाँ कुछ सदियों बाद संगम कविताएँ सरदारियों, व्यापार और कस्बों का वर्णन करती हैं। महापाषाण बनाने वाले उसी दुनिया के नीचे के लोग हैं। |
| 2:20 | क्या ये वही लोग हैं? बहुत से विद्वान महापाषाण समुदायों से संगम युग की सरदारियों तक सीधी रेखा देखते हैं, और स्थलों, मृद्भांड तथा लोहे के काम में निरंतरता की ओर इशारा करते हैं। दूसरे सावधानी बरतने को कहते हैं: प्रमाणों में एक अंतराल है, और वस्तुओं की निरंतरता लोगों या संस्थाओं की निरंतरता जैसी बात नहीं है। यह जोड़ उचित है। सिद्ध नहीं है। |
| 2:50 | अब याद रखने की तरकीब, पत्थर में खुदी हुई। मृतकों के ऊपर पत्थर। उनके पास लोहा। |
| 3:20 | खुदाई प्रश्नोत्तरी। रुकिए और शवाधान का नाम बताइए। पहला: ज़मीन के ऊपर खड़ी पट्टियाँ, जो एक शिला सँभाले हैं। डॉल्मेन। दूसरा: अकेला ऊँचा पत्थर, निशान के तौर पर खड़ा। मेनहिर। तीसरा: अवशेष एक बड़े मटके में, ज़मीन में गड़े हुए। कलश-शवाधान। मुख्य परीक्षा में एक उपयोगी सवाल पूछता है कि महापाषाण शवाधान आरंभिक दक्षिण के समाज के बारे में क्या बताते हैं। कब्र की सामग्री का इस्तेमाल कीजिए: काफ़ी मात्रा में लोहा, कब्रों के बीच अंतर जो हैसियत के अंतर की ओर इशारा करते हैं, और एक बहुत बड़े क्षेत्र में साझा शवाधान परंपरा। |
