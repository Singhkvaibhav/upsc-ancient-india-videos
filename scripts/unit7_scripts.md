# Ancient India, Simply Unearthed — Unit 7: Early medieval kingdoms

Episodes 35 to 37. Same format as `sample_ep04.md`.

**Rules applied in this unit.** No boundaries, no outline of India, no kingdom drawn as shaded territory. Temples are architecture only, with every sanctum a dark, empty opening; no deity or revered figure is shown. Rulers are generic carved figures with nameplates. Rajput origins are presented as a live scholarly dispute with each view given equal space, and no community is described as inherently martial or otherwise.

| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: stone, trowel, palm-leaf and staff light; stone brightest. | The Cholas are the best-documented ancient Indian state, because they wrote on walls. | 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: a carved board of the Tamil country and the sea, coasts and rivers only, with pins at Thanjavur and Gangaikonda Cholapuram; a dynasty band beside it. | The dynasty rises from about 850 CE under Vijayalaya and reaches its height under two rulers. Rajaraja the First, who built the Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur and conducted a survey of land for revenue. And his son Rajendra the First, who campaigned north to the Ganga and founded a new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram — a name that means the city of the Chola who took the Ganga. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved coastline with ships, a route line running east, pins at distant ports with no borders drawn. | And then something no other Indian dynasty of this period did: they built a navy and used it. Rajendra sent a naval expedition against Srivijaya, the maritime power of Southeast Asia, and the Cholas maintained trade and diplomatic contact with China. Historians debate the aims — protecting merchant guilds' access to eastern trade is the usual explanation — but the expedition itself is well attested. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: three carved assembly scenes in a row, each labelled; then a temple wall with inscription panels showing rules. | Now the administration, which is what examiners ask about. Three kinds of local assembly. The ur, the general assembly of an ordinary village. The sabha, the assembly of a brahmadeya, a village granted to Brahmins. And the nagaram, the assembly of a merchant town. Their proceedings were inscribed on temple walls, which is why we know how they worked. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved panel of the kudavolai process — pots, palm-leaf tickets, a drawn ticket, committee boards. | One inscription in particular, from Uttaramerur, describes the sabha's procedure in detail: the village divided into wards, candidates qualified by property, age, literacy and conduct, names written on palm leaves, put into a pot, and a ticket drawn out — the kudavolai system — with committees formed for gardens, tanks, gold and justice. It also disqualifies anyone who has not submitted accounts. Whether every village worked this way is unknown; that one village wrote its rules on a wall is certain. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "A strong centralised empire with a real bureaucracy, revenue survey and standing army." Right: "A segmentary state: ritual sovereignty at the centre, real power local, with assemblies and chiefs running their own affairs." | How centralised was it? One view reads the surveys, the inscriptions and the campaigns as evidence of a genuinely centralised empire. Another, influential since the 1980s, describes it as a segmentary state — the king's authority ritual and symbolic at a distance, with real administration done locally. A third position sits between them, accepting strong local institutions alongside effective central demands for revenue and soldiers. Know all three and the evidence each leans on. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: the chisel cuts "UR · SABHA · NAGARAM", then verdigris fills the grooves. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. Ur, the ordinary village. Sabha, the Brahmin village. Nagaram, the merchant town. |

Second: the temple built at Thanjavur by Rajaraja the First. The Brihadisvara temple. Third: the Southeast Asian maritime power against which Rajendra sent a naval expedition. Srivijaya. In Mains, a frequent question asks about Chola local self-government. Describe the three assemblies and the Uttaramerur procedure, then be careful: one inscription is evidence of a system's ideal, not proof of universal practice. |

## Episode 36 — Later Chalukyas, Hoysalas, Yadavas and Kakatiyas

Tag: "A plan, not a picture". | The brush uncovers a floor plan, and it is not a rectangle. It is a star, its points stepping around and around the shrine. A star plan gives a building far more wall surface than a square one would — and if your stone is soft enough to carve like wood, more surface means more room for carving. |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: stone, trowel and palm-leaf light. | Inscriptions, in Kannada, Telugu and Sanskrit, in large numbers. The standing temples, which are the main evidence. And literature, which for this period and region is substantial. |
| 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: a schematic Deccan board, rivers and ranges, with four pins — Kalyani, Dwarasamudra, Devagiri, Warangal — each with a dynasty nameplate. | Four kingdoms, and the easiest way to hold them is by capital. The Later Chalukyas, also called the Western Chalukyas, at Kalyani from about 973 CE. The Hoysalas at Dwarasamudra. The Yadavas, or Seunas, at Devagiri. And the Kakatiyas at Warangal. The last three rise as the Later Chalukyas decline, and all of them are ended in the early fourteenth century by invasions from the north. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved temple elevation of a Hoysala shrine, many-angled, with bands of ornament. Architecture only; the sanctum is a dark opening. | The Hoysalas are the ones to know for architecture. Their temples at Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura are built on star plans, on raised platforms, in soapstone — a stone soft enough to be worked almost like wood, and which hardens with exposure. The result is carving of extraordinary density: horizontal bands running right round the building, each with its own subject. Several of their sculptors signed their work, which is rare. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved tank and sluice system, fields below, a stepped embankment, with an inscription slab beside it. | The Kakatiyas matter for something less often taught and more useful: water. The Telangana plateau has no great river running through it, so the Kakatiyas and their chiefs built tanks — thousands of them, bunded reservoirs catching the monsoon and releasing it through sluices into fields below. Inscriptions record their construction and their upkeep. It is one of the clearest cases in ancient India of a state's power resting on an engineering decision. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved panel of a fortified hill with concentric walls; then a trade scene with merchant-guild nameplates. | Two more things. Devagiri, the Yadava capital, is a fortress on an isolated hill with concentric defences, considered near-impregnable in its day. And across all four kingdoms the merchant guilds were powerful — the Ayyavole guild in particular appears in inscriptions across the Deccan and beyond, which tells you that long-distance trade carried on regardless of which dynasty held which capital. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Old name, new name": "Dwarasamudra → Halebidu". Smaller tags: "Devagiri → Daulatabad · Warangal, still Warangal · Kalyani → Basavakalyan". | Names to match. Dwarasamudra is Halebidu in Karnataka. Devagiri was later renamed Daulatabad. Kalyani is Basavakalyan. Warangal is still Warangal. Questions often give the old name and expect the modern one, or the other way round. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: the chisel cuts "KALYANI · DWARASAMUDRA · DEVAGIRI · WARANGAL", then verdigris fills the grooves. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. Four capitals, four kingdoms. Kalyani, Chalukya. Dwarasamudra, Hoysala. Devagiri, Yadava. Warangal, Kakatiya. |
| 3:20 | Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three carved cards — a star-shaped plan; a bunded tank with a sluice; a hill fort with rings of wall. Then a Mains card. | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. First: the dynasty whose temples are built on star plans in soapstone at Belur and Halebidu. The Hoysalas. Second: the bunded reservoirs built in their thousands on the Telangana plateau. Tanks, built under the Kakatiyas. Third: the Yadava capital, a fortress on an isolated hill. Devagiri. In Mains, a good question asks how regional kingdoms shaped Indian art and engineering. Use Hoysala soapstone carving and Kakatiya tank irrigation together: one is about surplus and display, the other about making the surplus possible. |
