# Ancient India, Simply Unearthed: sample script

This is the **format sample** for the Ancient India series. Write every new script in exactly this format.

## Episode 4 — Harappan cities ★

**Inscription tip:** "WEST IS HIGH · EAST IS WIDE · BRICKS 1 : 2 : 4" · **Sources lit:** dug up (only) · **Timeline ribbon:** c. 2600–1900 BCE · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: were the "granaries" really granaries? · **Exhibit:** a standard 1:2:4 brick and a covered drain section · **Label facts:** c. 2600–1900 BCE · Citadel west, lower town east · Standard bricks, covered drains

| Time | Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | The Dig | DIG: a soil trench in cross-section under lamplight. A brush sweeps dust away to reveal a brick-lined drain. Small tags: "Harappa 1921", "Mohenjo-daro 1922". | A brush sweeps away four and a half thousand years of dust. Underneath lies a brick drain, still in place. In 1921 and 1922, archaeologists began uncovering two forgotten cities, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. How did people so long ago build cities this well planned? |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: five carved icons in a row; only the trowel ("dug up") lights. The palm-leaf icon shows a seal with unreadable signs and the label "script not yet deciphered". | How do we know? Almost entirely by digging. The Harappans did write, on seals, but no one has yet deciphered their script. So every fact in this episode comes from archaeology. |
| 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: the carved panel begins. A river runs along the bottom edge. A city in two parts: a small raised platform on the left labelled "Citadel · west · higher", a larger spread on the right labelled "Lower town · east · wider". The ribbon marker sits at c. 2600–1900 BCE. | The Harappan civilisation flourished from about 2600 to 1900 BCE, across a vast region along the Indus and its neighbouring rivers. Its cities followed a common plan. Most were divided in two. To the west stood the citadel: smaller, but built higher on a raised platform. To the east lay the lower town: larger, where most people lived. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: streets carved as a grid crossing at right angles. A single brick lifts out and shows its proportions "1 : 2 : 4", then copies of it appear in two distant towns. | Streets were laid out in a rough grid, crossing at right angles. And the bricks were standardised. Whether in Harappa or in a town hundreds of kilometres away, bricks kept the same proportions: one unit thick, two wide and four long. |
| 1:20 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a street in cutaway with a covered drain beneath; small house drains join it. A house around a courtyard with a well and a bathroom; its door opens onto a side lane. | The drainage system was remarkable. Drains ran along the streets, covered with bricks or stone slabs, and house drains connected to them. Houses were built around courtyards, and many had their own wells and bathrooms. Doors usually opened onto side lanes, not the main streets. |
| 1:45 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: the Great Bath in the citadel, a stepped rectangular tank labelled "about 12 m × 7 m". Then a large block of rooms labelled "granary?". | In the citadel of Mohenjo-daro stands the Great Bath: a tank about twelve metres long and seven metres wide, with steps at each end, made watertight with carefully laid bricks. Many scholars think it was used for ritual bathing. Nearby, and at Harappa, archaeologists found large structures they called granaries. |
| 2:05 | The Frieze | FRIEZE ends on a schematic physical board (rivers and coast, no borders) with four pins: Dholavira, Lothal, Kalibangan, Rakhigarhi. | In India today, you can visit Harappan sites such as Dholavira and Lothal in Gujarat, Kalibangan in Rajasthan, and Rakhigarhi in Haryana. Dholavira is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: two carved tablets side by side under the title "Scholars disagree". Left: "Granaries: stores for grain from tax or trade". Right: "Halls or warehouses: the evidence for grain is thin". Both stay level. | But were those really granaries? Some scholars see them as stores for grain, collected as tax or through trade. Others argue the evidence for stored grain is thin, and suggest they may have been halls or warehouses. The evidence allows both readings. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: a stone tablet; a chisel taps the words in letter by letter: "WEST IS HIGH · EAST IS WIDE · BRICKS 1 : 2 : 4". The grooves fill with verdigris green. Chisel taps. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. West is high, east is wide. And every brick is one, two, four. |
| 3:20 | Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three carved artefact cards, one at a time, each with "Pause and name it" and a 3-2-1 ring, then the answer: Great Bath → Mohenjo-daro; a basin with a channel → Lothal; a city in three parts with reservoirs → Dholavira. Then a Mains card. | Dig Quiz. Pause and name the site. First: a large watertight tank with steps at both ends. The Great Bath, at Mohenjo-daro. Second: a brick basin linked by a channel to a river, near the Gulf of Khambhat. The dockyard at Lothal. Third: a city in three parts, with great stone reservoirs, in the Rann of Kutch. Dholavira. In Mains, a classic question asks what Harappan town planning tells us about how the cities were run. Use the grid, the standard bricks and the drains as evidence of planning on a large scale. |
| 4:30 | The Gallery | GALLERY: the museum hall, warm lamplight. The brick and drain section are placed in a glass case. A museum label shows the three facts, then "Next gallery: Harappan life". | A standard brick and a drain take their place in the gallery: a city planned four and a half thousand years ago. Next gallery: how the Harappans lived, traded and worshipped. |

Fact check before publishing: the Great Bath's dimensions (about 11.9 m × 7 m) and the discovery years (Harappa 1921, Daya Ram Sahni; Mohenjo-daro 1922, R. D. Banerji).
