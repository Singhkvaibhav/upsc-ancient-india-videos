This is applied mathematics, done because a ritual required exactness. |

The system travels to the Arab world, where scholars develop it further, and reaches Europe from there. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: an astronomer's panel — a gnomon's shadow, an eclipse diagram with shadows, a star circle; then a later nameplate. | Astronomy. Both were compiled over long periods, so dating them precisely is not possible. |

Give the Gwalior inscription as the firmly dated case, mention the Bakhshali argument, and say the question is open. |

Somebody walked the edge of a village, agreed where it ran, and set stones into the ground so the agreement would outlast the memory of it. A boundary stone is a small object with a large implication: somebody now owns what is inside it, and the question of who has been one of the great arguments in Indian history. |

| 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: a carved plate with its clauses labelled one by one as they light — the donor, the recipient, the village, the boundaries, the immunities, the curse. | Start with what a grant actually says, because the clauses are the evidence. The donor, usually a king. The recipient — a Brahmin, a temple, a monastery, or an official. The village or land granted, with its boundaries described. The immunities: freedom from specified taxes, and often the right to collect them instead. Sometimes the right to administer, to judge, even to keep fines. And at the end, frequently, a curse on anyone who revokes it. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved sequence showing revenue flowing to the king, then diverted to an intermediary, then the intermediary growing. | Now the argument. If a king grants away the revenue of a village in perpetuity, he has permanently reduced his own income. If he grants the right to administer it, he has reduced his own authority. Do that a thousand times over several centuries and you have a state that no longer collects from, or governs, large parts of its own territory — and a layer of intermediaries between the king and the peasant who did not exist before. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved panel listing the feudalism case — declining trade, fewer coins, shrinking towns, peasants tied to land, self-sufficient villages. | The historians who call this Indian feudalism, R. S. Sharma foremost among them, build a wider case. They argue that long-distance trade declined after the Gupta period, that fewer coins were struck, that towns shrank, that villages became self-sufficient, and that cultivators became tied to the land under their new masters. On this reading the whole early medieval period is a feudal order, comparable in structure if not in detail to medieval Europe. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a counter-panel — coin finds, active ports, temple towns growing, merchant guild nameplates, regional state diagrams. | The critics answer point by point. Coin finds and port activity suggest trade continued, with its routes and its goods changing rather than collapsing. Temple towns grew in the south in exactly this period. Merchant guilds remained powerful. And they argue the European template imports the wrong questions: what the evidence shows is the formation of regional states with their own institutions — the integrative polity model, and in the far south the segmentary state model we met in the Chola episode. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "Indian feudalism: grants devolved revenue and authority, trade and towns declined, peasants were subjected." You are not expected to settle it either. You are expected to know what a land grant contains, what each side argues, which evidence each relies on, and to notice that both sides are reading the same documents. A good answer names the historians, sets out both cases, and takes a position with reasons rather than pretending there is a consensus. |

| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: two carved shelves side by side, one labelled what law books prescribe, one labelled what inscriptions record. | Now the distinction that organises the whole topic. The law books prescribe. They tell you what their authors thought should happen — restrictions on inheritance, on remarriage, on independence, lowering the age of marriage, and in one much-quoted passage the claim that a woman should not be independent at any stage of life. Inscriptions record. They show women owning property, making donations, endowing temples, and acting as patrons. Both are evidence; they are evidence of different things. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved timeline with markers at four periods, each carrying a short note; no single downward arrow. | What changes over time? The broad picture most textbooks give is of narrowing: women's participation in ritual and learning more visible in the earlier Vedic material, more restricted in the later; property rights limited in the law books, with stridhana — a woman's own property — recognised but bounded; the age of marriage falling. But it is not one smooth line. The Buddhist and Jain orders admitted women, with additional rules. Donor inscriptions continue throughout. And the evidence differs by region, period and social position. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved panel of different lives — a queen's nameplate, a nun's cell, a donor's plate, a weaver's loom, a field. | And remember who is invisible. Women who farmed, wove, carried water or worked in households appear rarely, and usually only in passing. A claim about "the position of women in ancient India" is really a claim about the small minority the sources let us see. |
