# Ancient India, Simply Unearthed — Unit 3: The age of the Buddha

Episodes 12 to 17. Same format as `sample_ep04.md`.

**The rule that governs this whole unit.** The Buddha, Mahavira and the Tirthankaras are **never shown as faces or figures**, in any frame. They are shown the way the earliest Indian art showed them: an empty throne, a pair of footprints, the Bodhi tree, a wheel, a stupa, a lotus, a parasol. This is not only a content rule — it is historically what aniconic early Buddhist art at Sanchi and Bharhut actually did, and the episodes say so. Rulers and ordinary people are generic carved figures with nameplates and no real likenesses.

**Also applied:** no boundaries or shaded kingdoms on any board; the Sarnath lion capital is never drawn; teachings are stated as the traditions state them, and historical claims are kept separate from doctrinal ones.

The Vajji confederacy, centred on Vaishali, is the famous one. The Buddha himself came from one of these, the Shakya ganasangha. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a city in cutaway — rampart, moat, gateway, streets, a market, and a new pottery type on a shelf. | The second urbanisation, as historians call it, is the return of city life to the subcontinent — this time in the Ganga plains rather than the Indus. Where you find it, you are usually looking at a town with money in it. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a chain of causes carved as linked panels — iron ploughs, rice transplanting, surplus, trade, guilds, coins, taxes, standing armies. | Why now? Surplus feeds people who are not farmers — craftsmen, traders, soldiers, officials. Trade needs a medium, so punch-marked coins appear. Trade organises itself into guilds. And a king who can tax a surplus can pay a standing army, which is how four states end up eating twelve. |

## Episode 13 — The rise of Magadha

**Inscription tip:** "IRON · RIVERS · ELEPHANTS · TWO CAPITALS" · **Sources lit:** texts, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** c. 544 – 321 BCE · **Plaque:** Old name, new name: "Pataliputra → Patna" · **Exhibit:** a section of fortified city wall · **Label facts:** Haryanka, Shishunaga, Nanda · Rajagriha, then Pataliputra · Geography did much of the work

Somebody had a great deal of money, and a reason to be afraid. This episode is about how one state out of sixteen ended up with both. |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: palm-leaf and trowel light. | Buddhist and Jain texts, which were written close to these events and name the kings, and excavation at the capitals. | 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: a schematic board — two rivers converging, hills ringing a site, a forest band, an iron-ore hatching, and elephants at the forest edge. Its first capital was ringed by hills, which is a fortress you do not have to build. It had iron ore nearby, for weapons and tools. It had forests, for timber and for elephants — and war elephants were an advantage no neighbour could easily match. And it had the most fertile stretch of the Ganga plain, which means surplus, which means taxes. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved dynasty band — Haryanka, with nameplates: Bimbisara, Ajatashatru, Udayin. Generic figures, no likenesses. | Then the rulers. The Haryanka dynasty comes first. Bimbisara expands by marriage alliances and by annexing Anga. His son Ajatashatru takes a harder line: he fights Kosala and breaks the Vajji confederacy, which takes years because a confederacy is hard to defeat in one battle. Udayin, who follows, is credited with founding a fort at Pataliputra, where the rivers meet. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: the Shishunaga band, then the Nanda band, the latter carved larger, with a treasury and a mass of troops. | The Shishunagas follow, and are remembered chiefly for destroying the power of Avanti — removing Magadha's last serious rival. Then the Nandas, who are the ones the Greek accounts noticed. They built an enormous army and a famously large treasury, and they were widely disliked for the taxation that paid for both. When Alexander's soldiers heard what waited for them beyond the Beas, they refused to go further. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: two capitals side by side — Rajagriha in its ring of hills, Pataliputra at the river junction with ramparts along the bank. | Watch the capital move, because it tells the story. Rajagriha is defensive: a city inside a ring of hills, right for a state that is still vulnerable. Pataliputra is offensive and commercial: set where the rivers meet, it controls traffic and projects power downstream and up. A state that moves its capital from the hills to the river has stopped worrying about surviving and started thinking about reach. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Old name, new name": "Pataliputra → Patna". Smaller tag: "Rajagriha → Rajgir". | Pataliputra is Patna. Rajagriha is Rajgir. Both are in Bihar, and both are places you can still visit. |
