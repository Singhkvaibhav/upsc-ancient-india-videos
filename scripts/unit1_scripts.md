# Ancient India, Simply Unearthed — Unit 1: Prehistory and the Harappans

Episodes 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6. Episode 4 (Harappan cities) is the format sample and is already done.

Written in exactly the format of `sample_ep04.md`: a header line, then a Time | Segment | Visual | Narration table following the seven segments, then the Hindi narration table.

**Series rules applied throughout:** no boundaries, no outline of India, no shaded territories — sites are pins on a schematic physical board carrying "Schematic · not to scale · boundaries not shown". Nothing revered is drawn. Uncertain dates carry "about" or "c.". Where scholars genuinely disagree, the plaque gives both readings equal space and takes no side.

| 0:00 | The Dig | DIG: a deep trench, strata sharply layered. The brush reveals a large teardrop-shaped hand axe. Not a factory, not a workshop — a person, sitting down, striking one rock with another until it did what they wanted. This is the oldest technology in India, and it ran for hundreds of thousands of years. |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: only the trowel lights, strongly. The other four stay dark. | For this episode there is one source and one only. Nobody wrote anything. Nobody carved an inscription. Everything we know, we know because it was dug out of the ground. |

First, the Palaeolithic, the old stone age: big tools struck from stone cores — hand axes, cleavers, choppers. People hunted and gathered, moved with the seasons, and lived in caves and open camps. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: the painted rock shelters, drawn as carved panels of hunting scenes and animals, with a small tag "Bhimbetka · UNESCO". | And they painted. At Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh there are hundreds of rock shelters whose walls carry paintings of animals, hunts and dances, made across a very long span of time. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and it is the closest thing we have to the imagination of the first Indians. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: the Mesolithic band. Tiny blades, no bigger than a fingernail, carved oversized; then shown hafted in a row along a wooden shaft. | Then the Mesolithic, the middle stone age. The tools get small — microliths, tiny blades, often set in a row along a handle of wood or bone to make a cutting edge or a barbed point. Smaller tools meant finer work and better hunting, and some groups began to keep animals. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: the Neolithic band — ground and polished tools, pots, a granary pit, ears of barley and wheat; then three site vignettes. | Then the Neolithic, the new stone age, and the change that matters more than any other in human history. People began to farm. Tools are now ground and polished instead of chipped. Pottery appears, and storage, and permanent houses. At Mehrgarh, in the Bolan valley, there is evidence of wheat and barley farming and herded animals from about 7000 BCE. At Burzahom in the Kashmir valley, people dug pit dwellings into the ground. And at Koldihwa in the Belan valley, there is evidence of rice. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "Some Bhimbetka paintings may be Upper Palaeolithic." Right: "Most surviving paintings are Mesolithic or later." Equal tablets. | How old is the art at Bhimbetka? Some scholars argue the oldest layers reach back into the Upper Palaeolithic. Others hold that most of what survives is Mesolithic or later, because paintings were made again and again on the same walls over thousands of years. Dating paint on rock is genuinely hard, and the honest answer is a range, not a date. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: the chisel cuts "BIG STONE · SMALL STONE · SEED", then the grooves glow verdigris. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. Three ages, three words. Big stone, small stone, seed. |
| 3:20 | Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three carved cards — a hand axe; a row of microliths in a shaft; a polished axe beside grain. Then a Mains card. | Dig Quiz. Pause and name the age.
