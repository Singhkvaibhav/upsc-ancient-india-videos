The traditions say he was helped by Kautilya, also called Chanakya or Vishnugupta, a Brahmin teacher from Taxila who became his minister. | 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: two carved figures facing each other across a treaty tablet, elephants carved beside one of them. | Around 305 BCE he met Seleucus Nicator, who ruled the eastern part of Alexander's empire. A marriage alliance is mentioned too, though the details are unclear. Seleucus then sent Megasthenes as his ambassador to Pataliputra, which is why we have a Greek description of the Mauryan capital at all. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved diagram — seven elements of the state in a ring, each labelled, lighting in turn. | And then the Arthashastra, attributed to Kautilya, which is where the theory lives. Its most quoted idea is the saptanga, the seven limbs of the state: the king, the ministers, the territory and people, the fort, the treasury, the army, and the ally. A state, it argues, is healthy only when all seven are. Its other famous idea is the mandala theory of foreign policy: your neighbour is your likely enemy, and your neighbour's neighbour is your likely friend. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "Largely Kautilya's own work, from the Mauryan period." Others argue it reached its present form several centuries later, as a compilation, and that using it to describe Chandragupta's government is reading a later textbook back onto an earlier state. Most now take a middle position: an older core, with later additions. Treat it as political theory first, and as evidence of practice only with care. |

In Mains, a good question asks how far the Arthashastra can be used as evidence for Mauryan administration. Answer it as a question about dating and genre, not just content. |

## Episode 19 — Ashoka and dhamma

**Inscription tip:** "ONE WAR, THEN NONE" · **Sources lit:** inscriptions, texts, travellers · **Timeline ribbon:** c. 268 – 232 BCE · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: was dhamma a religion or a policy? · **Exhibit:** the Rampurva bull capital · **Label facts:** Kalinga, about 261 BCE · Dhamma, stated in his own words · Dhamma-mahamatras appointed

| Time | Segment | Visual | Narration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00 | The Dig | DIG: the brush reveals a polished stone capital carved as a standing bull on a bell-shaped lotus base. Tag: "Rampurva · a pillar capital". | The brush uncovers a bull, carved on a polished stone capital that once stood on top of a pillar. It is Mauryan, and the polish on it is so fine that for a long time people argued about how it was done. The king who put these up left something rarer than monuments, though — he left his own words. |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: stone, palm-leaf and staff light; the stone icon brightest. | This is the first Indian ruler we can quote directly. His edicts are carved on rocks and pillars across the subcontinent, in the first person. Buddhist tradition adds a great deal, and later travellers describe the sites. When the edicts and the tradition disagree, historians go with the edicts. |
| 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: a carved panel — a succession scene with nameplates, then a pillar being raised. Generic figures. | Ashoka came to the throne about 268 BCE, the grandson of Chandragupta. Buddhist tradition says the succession was contested and violent. The edicts say nothing about that, which is itself worth noticing: he tells us what he wants us to know. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a schematic board with a single pin on the eastern coast labelled Kalinga; then a carved edict tablet with lines of script. No battle scene, no bodies. | Then Kalinga, about 261 BCE. His own thirteenth rock edict gives the figures: a hundred and fifty thousand deported, a hundred thousand killed, and many times that number dead afterwards. And then it says something no other ancient conqueror says in his own inscription — that he felt remorse, deep sorrow and regret. A king carved his own change of heart into rock and left it where everyone could read it. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved list of dhamma's contents, each line appearing in turn. | After that he promotes what he calls dhamma. Read the edicts and you find what he means by it: obedience to parents, respect for teachers, generosity to Brahmins and ascetics alike, kindness to servants, truthfulness, restraint in spending, and non-injury to living beings. Also tolerance — one edict says plainly that honouring your own sect while disparaging another's harms your own. It is strikingly practical, and strikingly non-sectarian. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: officials with nameplates travelling; wells, rest houses, trees along a road; a hospital tag. | And he built an administration for it. He appointed dhamma-mahamatras, officers whose job was to promote it and look after the welfare of different communities. He replaced royal hunting tours with dhamma-yatras, tours of inspection and teaching. He records planting trees along roads, digging wells, building rest houses, and arranging medical treatment for people and animals. He also sent missions abroad, including to Sri Lanka and to Hellenistic kings he names. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "Dhamma is his personal Buddhism, offered to his subjects." Right: "Dhamma is a civic ethic, deliberately non-sectarian, for holding a diverse empire together." | What was dhamma, then? One reading takes it as his Buddhism, made public: he was a lay Buddhist, he says so, and the content overlaps. The other reading notes that the edicts addressed to the general public never mention the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path or nirvana, and argues dhamma was a deliberately non-sectarian civic ethic for governing an empire of many communities. The evidence genuinely supports both emphases, and good answers say so. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: the chisel cuts "ONE WAR, THEN NONE", then verdigris fills the grooves. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. One war, then none. Kalinga is the hinge of the reign. |
| 3:20 | Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three carved cards — an edict tablet with a coastal pin; an officer's nameplate; a road with trees and a well. Then a Mains card. | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. First: the war of about 261 BCE that he describes with remorse in his own edict. Kalinga. Second: the officers he appointed to promote dhamma and look after communities. The dhamma-mahamatras. Third: the tours of inspection and teaching that replaced royal hunting trips. Dhamma-yatras. In Mains, a frequent question asks what Ashoka's dhamma was. Quote the edicts for its content, give both scholarly readings, and note what the public edicts leave out. |

**Inscription tip:** "FOUR PROVINCES · ONE CAPITAL" · **Sources lit:** texts, travellers, inscriptions, dug up · **Timeline ribbon:** c. 321 – 185 BCE · **Plaque:** Scholars disagree: why did the Mauryan empire fall? · **Exhibit:** a polished stone pillar section · **Label facts:** Four provincial capitals · Megasthenes' Indica survives in fragments · Ended about 185 BCE

| 0:00 | The Dig | DIG: the brush reveals a section of polished sandstone column, the surface so smooth it holds the lamplight. Tag: "Mauryan polish". | The brush uncovers a drum of sandstone, and the surface is extraordinary — polished to a mirror, holding the lamplight like glass. It is the signature finish of Mauryan stonework. An empire that can do this to stone can do other difficult things too, and this episode is about those. |
| 0:20 | How do we know? | SOURCES: palm-leaf, staff, stone and trowel all light. | Four sources. The Arthashastra, for theory. Megasthenes' Indica, for an outsider's description — though it survives only as fragments quoted by later Greek writers, which is a real limitation. The edicts, for administration in the ruler's own words. And excavation at Pataliputra and elsewhere. |
| 0:35 | The Frieze | FRIEZE: a carved diagram — a central capital with four provincial centres around it, each with a nameplate; officials below each. | Start with the shape of it. The empire was governed from Pataliputra, with provinces under governors who were often princes of the royal family. Four provincial capitals are usually named: Taxila in the north-west, Ujjain in the west, Suvarnagiri in the south, and Tosali in the east. Below them, districts and villages, with officers the edicts name: rajukas, who had authority over large rural areas, pradeshikas, and yuktas. |
| 1:00 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved city plan with committees as labelled panels, a palisade wall, and officials at a gate. | Megasthenes describes Pataliputra as a great city on the river, defended by a timber palisade with hundreds of towers and many gates — and excavation has in fact found massive wooden palisade remains, which is a satisfying case of a text and the ground agreeing. He describes its administration by committees handling crafts, foreigners, births and deaths, trade, manufactures and tax collection. How literally to take his numbers is debated; the structure is plausible, the detail is secondhand. |
| 1:25 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: carved panels for revenue — fields with a share marked off, irrigation works, mines, a state workshop, a dam tag reading Sudarshana. | And the money. Land revenue was the backbone, a share of the produce, assessed and collected by a salaried bureaucracy rather than farmed out. The state ran mines, forests and workshops directly. It invested in irrigation: the Sudarshana lake in Saurashtra was built under the Mauryas, and we know that because a later inscription records repairing it. A standing army and a large officialdom were paid out of this, which is exactly what the Nandas had shown was possible, on a far bigger scale. |
| 1:50 | The Frieze | FRIEZE pans: a carved succession line thinning out; then a final nameplate, Brihadratha; then a new one, Pushyamitra Shunga. | After Ashoka's death about 232 BCE, the empire shrinks quickly. The later Mauryas are barely known. The last, Brihadratha, was killed about 185 BCE by his own commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga, who founded the next dynasty. From the first unification of the subcontinent to the end took about a hundred and forty years. |
| 2:20 | The Plaque | PLAQUE: "Scholars disagree". Left: "A fiscal and administrative problem: a huge salaried bureaucracy and army outran the revenue, and over-centralisation left no strength in the provinces." Right: "A political one: weak successors, provincial revolts and pressure in the north-west; dhamma and pacifism are not an adequate explanation." | Why did it fall? The explanations divide. One group of arguments is economic and administrative: an empire this centralised needed constant revenue and able kings, and had neither for long; the provinces had no independent strength to fall back on. Another is political: weak successors, revolts, and pressure from the north-west. An older view blamed Ashoka's pacifism and his patronage of Buddhism for weakening the state — that one is now largely rejected, because the empire outlasted him by half a century and the edicts show no dismantling of the army. Say which explanation you prefer and why. |
| 2:50 | Inscription | INSCRIPTION: the chisel cuts "FOUR PROVINCES · ONE CAPITAL", then verdigris fills the grooves. | Here is the memory tip, carved in stone. Taxila, Ujjain, Suvarnagiri, Tosali — four provinces, one capital at Pataliputra. |
| 3:20 | Dig Quiz | QUIZ: three carved cards — a rural officer's nameplate; a lake and dam; a fragment of a Greek scroll. Then a Mains card. | Dig Quiz. Pause and name it. First: the officers the edicts give authority over large rural areas. The rajukas. Second: the irrigation work in Saurashtra built under the Mauryas and repaired by later rulers. The Sudarshana lake. Third: the Greek ambassador's account of India, which survives only as fragments quoted by later writers. The Indica. In Mains, a frequent question asks why the Mauryan empire declined. Set out the administrative, economic and political explanations, say which you find strongest, and explain why the pacifism argument is no longer accepted. |
